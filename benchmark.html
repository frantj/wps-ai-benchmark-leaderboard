The WPS AI Benchmark

Gender mainstreaming is an operational requirement, not an ethical add-on

Why Women, Peace and Security matters to AI systems used in real operations, and why this benchmark is built the way it is. For how scenarios are validated, see Methodology.

Why WPS matters

UN Security Council Resolution 1325 (2000) established that women's full and equal participation in peace and security efforts, and attention to how conflict affects women and girls, is not a peripheral concern — it is core to whether missions succeed. NATO's 2024 WPS Policy makes the same case in operational terms: gender-responsive planning improves situational awareness, community trust, and force protection. Evidence-based, gender-sensitive analysis is treated as a driver of mission effectiveness, not a values statement layered on top of it.

AI systems are increasingly used to draft situation reports, plan community engagement, and analyze conflict dynamics — the same tasks WPS doctrine governs. If those systems default to gender-blind analysis, they quietly reintroduce the exact blind spot the WPS agenda exists to close.

The gap

WPS-specific data and analysis are underrepresented online, and what exists is thinning further as WPS-focused grants and specialist programs are cut. General-purpose AI evaluations don't fill the gap: independent research — including the Institute for Integrated Transitions' 2025 AI on the Frontline study of LLMs on real-world conflict-resolution scenarios — found that gendered and intersectional dynamics central to peace and security were largely absent from existing evaluation criteria, and that leading models averaged well below basic professional standards on due diligence and risk disclosure. General AI benchmarks simply aren't built to notice whether a model handles WPS content competently — because none of them are testing for it.

Why prompt tiers, and why sparse prompts are the real test

Every scenario is written at one of several difficulty tiers, from prompts that spell out the gender dimensions in full down to terse, practitioner-realistic requests — a flash report, a sitrep — that mention nothing about gender at all. Across every model generation tested, the pattern holds: performance is respectable when a prompt hands the model the WPS framing, and collapses on the sparse, real-world tier where nobody does. That collapse — not the score on an easy, cued prompt — is this benchmark's most credible finding, because sparse requests are what practitioners actually send.
